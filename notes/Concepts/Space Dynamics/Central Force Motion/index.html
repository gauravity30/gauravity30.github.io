Central Force Motion

Created on: 2025-01-03
Tags: #CentralForceMotion
Course: Space Dynamics

Equations of motion in an inertial frame

  • A system with two point mass under the influence of gravity observed from an inertial frame of reference.
  • The frame of reference may move with a constant speed, but the axes don’t rotate (otherwise acc. will be there and frame is non-inertial).

\(\vec{R_{G}} = \frac{m_{1}\vec{R_{1}} + m_{2}\vec{R_{2}}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}\) $\vec{R_{G}}$ is the position vector of the center of mass of the system. The velocity and acceleration of $G$ is given as \(\vec{R_{G}'} = \frac{m_{1}\vec{R_{1}'} + m_{2}\vec{R_{2}'}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}\) \(\vec{R_{G}''} = \frac{m_{1}\vec{R_{1}''} + m_{2}\vec{R_{2}''}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}\) The magnitude of force of gravitational attraction between $m_{2}$ and $m_{1}$ is given as \(F_{21} = F_{12}= \frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^2}\) By Newton’s third law, $\vec{F_{12}} = -\vec{F_{21}}$ while $\vec{F_{12}} = m_{1}\vec{R_{1}’’}$ and $\vec{F_{21}} = m_{2}\vec{R_{2}’’}$. Thus, $m_{1}\vec{R_{1}’’} + m_{2}\vec{R_{2}’’} = 0$.

Therefore, the acceleration of the center of mass $G$ of the system is zero. It moves with a constant velocity in a straight line.

Equations of relative motion

Let us define the position vector of $m_{2}$ relative to $m_{1}$ as $\vec{r}$ where \(\vec{r} = \vec{R_{2}}-\vec{R_{1}}\) Now, \(m_{1}m_{2}(\vec{R_{2}''}-\vec{R_{1}''}) = -\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^2} (m_{1}+m_{2}) \hat{u}_{r}\) i.e. \(\vec{r''} = - \frac{G(m_{1}+m_{2})}{r^2} \hat{u}_{r}\) Assuming $\mu = G(m_{1}+m_{2})$, we get \(\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r}\) This second order differential equation governs the motion of $m_{2}$ relative to $m_{1}$.

In the moving frame $xyz$, the relative vector $\mathbf{r}{rel}$, relative velocity $\dot{\mathbf{r}}{rel}$ and relative acceleration $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel}$ is given as \(\mathbf{r}_{rel}= x \hat{i} + y \hat{j} + z \hat{k}\)\(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel}= \dot{x} \hat{i} + \dot{y} \hat{j} + \dot{z} \hat{k}\)\(\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel}= \ddot{x} \hat{i} + \ddot{y} \hat{j} + \ddot{z} \hat{k}\) Whereas in absolute frame, \(\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel} + \Omega \times \mathbf{r}\) \(\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel} + \dot{\Omega} \times \mathbf{r} + \Omega \times(\Omega \times \mathbf{r}) + 2\Omega \times \dot{\mathbf{r}}_{rel}\)

Angular Momentum

The angular momentum of body $m_{2}$ relative to $m_{1}$ is the moment of $m_{2}$ ‘s relative linear momentum $m_{2}\mathbf{\dot{r}}$ \(\mathbf{H}_{2/1} = \mathbf{r} \times m_{2}\mathbf{\dot{r}}\) Now, h is the relative angular momentum of $m_{2}$ per unit mass, that is specific relative angular momentum. \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{\dot{r}}\) Taking the time derivative, \(\frac{d\mathbf{h}}{dt} = \mathbf{\dot{r}}\times\mathbf{\dot{r}} + \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{\ddot{r}}\) Now, cross multiplication of a vector with itself is zero and $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{\ddot{r}}$ can be written as \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{\ddot{r}} = \mathbf{r} \times \left(-\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r}\right) = 0\) Thus, \(\frac{d\mathbf{h}}{dt} = 0\) i.e. the specific relative angular momentum is constant and
